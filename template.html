Neighbours or diaspora?

Countries in the same corner of Europe give each other extra points. But is that because they are close, or because they share language, culture and emigrants? This page tests our own country clusters against distance, against random groupings and against the groups the votes form by themselves.

08 · METHOD

How this was measured

Favoritism

For each final: the share of its points a country gave another country, minus the share that country got from every other voter in the same final, multiplied by 58 (one set of 1–8, 10, 12). It reads as extra points. Since 2016 a country gives two sets (jury and public), up to 24 points to one country. Working in shares keeps those years comparable with earlier ones. Pairs need at least five shared finals (four in the shorter periods).

Distance

Straight-line distance between the countries' centres. Australia is left out of the distance charts, as it sits 13,000 km away from everyone.

Groups in the data

Countries are linked by how much they favour each other in both directions, and the Louvain method splits that network into the groups that are most tightly linked inside. It never sees the map, only the votes. Each group is named after its two most connected members. The split is the best of 40 runs and can change with the period.

Random clusters

The countries in our five clusters were reshuffled a thousand times into five clusters of the same sizes. If random clusters often reached the same favoritism as ours, ours would mean little.

What this cannot show

The votes show patterns, not reasons. Language, shared TV and music markets, emigrant communities and plain taste all produce the same pattern. The diaspora explanation fits the pairs in section 04 and the public-versus-jury split, but the votes alone cannot prove it.
